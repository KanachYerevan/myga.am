## The Standard that doesn't exist

In nearly every debate on urban planning in Armenia—whether inside City Hall, across neighborhood groups, or in local investigative reports—a single figure is cited with absolute certainty:

> "The World Health Organization mandates a minimum of 9 m² of green space per resident, with an ideal target of 50 m²."

Municipal officials frequently lean on this metric to present comforting statistics about citywide averages. Activists and residents cite it to demonstrate just how much greenery Yerevan has lost since the Soviet era.

Almost everyone treats it as an unquestionable, globally recognized benchmark for a healthy city.

There is only one problem: The World Health Organization has never published, recommended, or mandated a 9 m² per capita green space standard.

## Where did it come from?

How did an unverified number become the foundation of environmental policy and public debate?

The 9 m² standard is an urban legend passed down through academic papers and municipal reports. It is often mentioned in footnotes in local whitepapers or master plan justifications, they often point to another secondary article, which often doesn't even have the quote. Researchers and officials repeat what others wrote without verifying the source, creating decades of institutional telephone.

The 9 m² number actually comes from post-World War II planning rules in Italy—specifically Ministerial Decree No. 1444 of April 2, 1968 (Standard Urbanistici).  The law required that for every resident in new housing developments, at least 18 m² had to be reserved for public amenities, including 9 m² for public parks, sports  fields and recreation.

## What is the real requirement?

The WHO doesn't use per-person average, because that tells you nothing about who actually gets to enjoy the shade, or how far a family has to walk to find clean air.

City Hall can easily claim a healthy average of 15 m² per person simply by counting an empty, rocky hillside on the outer edge of the city. Meanwhile, families living in crowded apartment blocks have nothing outside their windows except asphalt and parked cars. If you have to take a 45-minute bus ride just to reach a proper park, that distant greenery does nothing to cool down your street or give your kids a safe place to play.

Official WHO Europe guidelines prioritize real-world access, scale, and walking distance:

- Proximity.  Every resident should be able to reach a quality public green space within a maximum walking distance of 300 metres (about a 5-minute walk) from their front door.
- Minimum size.  The park must be at least 0.5 hectares (5,000 m²). A tiny strip of grass between lanes of traffic or a single concrete planter cannot lower summer temperatures, muffle street noise, or give people a calm place to rest.

To turn health science into practical city design, urban forestry experts developed the 3-30-300 rule, pioneered by Prof. Cecil Konijnendijk:

- (3) Every resident should clearly see at least 3 mature trees from their window at home, school, or work.
- (30) Every neighborhood must have at least 30% tree canopy cover shading its streets and yards.
- (300) Residents should walk no more than 300 metres to reach a safe public park of at least 0.5 hectares.

## What does Yerevan state

Yerevan Municipality declares that in 2026, there is 8.4 m² of greep space per inhabitant, which is 93% of the recommended amount, very close.  After implementing the Dalma Garden project, the number will jump to at least 10.3 m².  But Dalma Garden is 3 km from the downtown, on straight line, or 4 km on road.  So this doesn't count towards WHO recommendation, unless you leave within 300 meters.

Also, what do you count as a green area?  According to Yerevan laws, that would be any open soil with any vegetation, wild or planted.  This means that the hills of Saralanj, with stones, sand and a few thorns, formally count as green area.

If you look at the definition of a park, that would be an area where tree canopy projection covers 40% of the land.  This is much better, and closer to WHO requirements (find corresponding European standard).  But when the official infographics say about 867.5 ha of green areas and 30% coverage, they clearly don't mean parks.  You won't find 1/3 of Yerevan covered by parks.

So what are the actual numbers?

## What is the actual state

WHO says that a residend should walk no more than 300 meters to reach a safe public park of at least 0.5 hectares.  The safety requirement disqualifies many wild areas like slopes of Hrazdan Gorge and Saralanj.  Let's see how good this works for Arabkir, the third district by population density.

| Location | Area | Qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| Mergelyan park | 0.64 ha | - |
| Nor-Arabkir park | 2.50 ha | + |
| Slavic garden | 0.43 ha | - |
| Vahagn Davtyan park | 2.24 ha | + |
| Adonts park | 1.83 ha | + |

That's all major parks.  Total area of Arabkir is 1329 ha.  Total area of qualifying parks is 6.57 ha, or 0.5%.
