This page is your guide to selecting trees and woody plants for planting in Yerevan, Armenia.
It combines species suited to the city's long, hot and dry summers, alkaline soils, and winter lows that regularly reach −10 to −15 °C with woody species recorded growing in Yerevan.
The list includes native trees of the Armenian highland alongside species long proven in the city's parks, streets, and courtyards.

## Understanding the data

1. H and W: the average height and width of a mature specimen, in metres. These are typical landscape dimensions rather than record sizes: a street pit or a dry courtyard keeps a tree smaller, while an irrigated park lets it exceed them. For climbers, H is the height reached on a support and W the spread along it.
2. E: a tick marks a species that holds its foliage through winter; an empty cell marks one that drops it. There is no third state here, so an empty cell means deciduous and not unknown. It matters beyond the look of the thing: under an evergreen canopy the L rating applies in February as well as in July, and a deciduous canopy gives summer shade with winter sun, the reverse of what you want from a screen. The three semi-evergreens here are left unmarked, because a cold Yerevan winter strips them.
3. Z: the coldest USDA hardiness zone in which the species is normally hardy outdoors. Yerevan is 7b, so 7 or lower can be expected to survive the winter, 8 is marginal, and 9 or warmer needs protection. An asterisk marks a species rated warmer than the city.
4. Water: the irrigation a species needs once established, on the four-class [WUCOLS](https://ucanr.edu/sites/WUCOLS/) scale: VL very low (1), L low (2), M moderate (3), H high (4). It measures demand, not drainage or flood tolerance. Yerevan receives about 300 mm of precipitation a year against a summer reference evapotranspiration above 1,200 mm, so every species needs watering through its first two or three summers; the class tells you what happens after that. Group plants within one class, or one step apart. Under a mature canopy the roots above take most of the rain, so establishment watering counts for more there than the class does.
5. Light: the shadiest exposure in which a species persists. S needs open sky and six or more hours of direct sun; SP tolerates a light or high canopy; P takes about half a day of sun, or all-day dapple; F persists in deep or north-facing shade for most of the day. The class is a floor, not a target: below its rating a species etiolates, leans and eventually dies, and above it most plants still do better. Read it against the canopy the site will carry in twenty years rather than the one it carries today, because a tree planted in the gap left by a felled elm comes back under its neighbours' crowns long before it is mature. A dagger marks a species that scorches in full summer sun and needs dappling or afternoon shade here. Ratings follow [Ellenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicator_value) light values for European species, _Silvics of North America_ for North American ones, and native habitat for the rest.
6. OPALS: the [Ogren Plant Allergy Scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogren_Plant_Allergy_Scale), from 1 (least allergenic) to 10 (most). A dash means no published score was found. Scores are compiled from [published OPALS ratings by Thomas Leo Ogren](https://archive.org/details/allergyfreegarde00thom/).
