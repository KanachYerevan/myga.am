The United Nations [defines a framework](https://land.unece.org/treesincities/policy-brief) for improving urban health and biodiversity. It measures how evenly tree canopy covers the city and how accessible green zones are. This has nothing to do with the "square meters per person" metric, which was never an official standard, but is still often [quoted by Yerevan](/journal/green-space-requirement/) and other cities.

The framework, called the "3-30-300 Rule", states that in a healthy environment, you should see 3 mature trees from your window, tree canopy should cover 30% of the area, and it should take less than a 300-meter walk to reach a safe, publicly accessible park of at least 0.5 hectares.

## Where we are now

Yerevan is far from meeting this recommendation. The biggest problem is that only 13% of the city has a suitable park within walking distance. Some districts have only 2–3 big parks, while others, like Norq Marash, have none at all. The advertised figure of 8.4 square meters per person does not help people in Norq Marash.

City regulations require new construction sites to set aside 40% of their land for green zones. But this rule has two problems. First, the official definition of a green zone includes any open soil with vegetation, whether wild or planted. So bare dirt with a few thorns counts. Even if the developer plants proper trees, no law requires anyone to maintain them, so the greenery quickly dies off after the apartments are sold and the builder moves to the next project.

## How this can be solved

First, the city needs a distributed network of small local parks. The municipality is doing good work demolishing illegal garages and other buildings that took over courtyards. However, the cleared space is usually turned into small playgrounds or sports courts. These are useful, but they do not replace parks. One cleared area is often 0.2 to 0.3 hectares, so coordinated planning across adjacent blocks can create local parks that meet modern standards and noticeably improve daily life.

Second, large new developments should not simply set aside leftover green plots that quickly become bare dirt. They need to create real parks instead. This can be cheaper for developers: instead of spreading 3.5 hectares of grass, they could build a proper one-hectare park that genuinely serves residents. If a project is too small to set aside 0.5 hectares of land, the developer should team up with neighboring projects to build a shared park before receiving a building permit.
